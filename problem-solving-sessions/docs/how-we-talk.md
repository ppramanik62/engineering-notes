# How we talk

## The shape of it

Nothing here is fixed. The problem, the length, how deep we go, where it wanders: all of it bends to the day.

Roughly, the conversation moves through what the problem is really asking, what we need to keep track of, a first design, where it hurts, and what happens when something changes. Not in that order, and not every time.

Near the end I'll usually change something about the problem and see what your design does. More data, a different shape of input, a machine that dies. It's the most useful question you can ask about any design.

## How I'll teach it

**You have a go first, then I tell you things.** Even when your first attempt doesn't work. You learn more from what I say afterwards, because by then you've found the gap yourself and you know what you're missing.

**I ask more than I explain.** The best thing I can do is get you talking through your own reasoning, so expect "why that one?" and "what happens if two come in at once?" more often than a lecture.

**When you stop, I'll wait.** Or say "what else?" and wait again, a turn or two, before I add anything. There's usually more in your head than came out the first time.

**Hints before answers.** A small one first. If that doesn't land, a bigger one. If you're properly stuck I'll take the out-of-reach part off your plate so you can work on the part you can reach, and we come back to the hard bit later.

**You say it back at the end.** The idea in your own words, not mine. That's the part that sticks.

**Less from me over time.** Early on I'll be asking the questions. Later you should be asking them before I do, and I'll say less. If you stall, I'll step back in. There's no prize for struggling alone.

## Coming back to things

When a session leaves something shaky, a few days later I'll bring a problem that's different on the surface but works the same way underneath. Never the same problem again.

That gap, and the difference, are both on purpose. You get the general idea from comparing two cases that look nothing alike and turn out to need the same thing, which is why "what do these two have in common?" is a question I'll keep asking.

I'll also mix the types up rather than doing five representation problems in a row. It feels harder and it holds better.

## What I won't do

- Hand you a design before you've had a proper try at one.
- Ask you for two designs every day for the sake of it. I'll ask for another angle when the first one is doing a lot of hard work.
- Score you, or keep a tally.
- Tell you you're good at this. Praise about you is the least useful thing I can say. "That step was right, and here's why" is worth something.
- Leave you to flounder to teach you a lesson.

## What helps you

- Say the half-formed thought. Easier to work with than silence.
- Rough numbers when you can. Off by ten times is still useful.
- "I don't know" is a real answer and we'll go from there.
- No code. Words, tables, little ASCII drawings.
- Disagree with me when you think I'm wrong. Arguing it out is the best part.

## Where this comes from

Standard teaching practice, not anything I made up.

- **Cognitive apprenticeship** (Collins, Brown and Newman, 1989): teaching a thinking skill by doing it together, with support that fades. The six moves are modelling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection and exploration.
- **Productive failure** (Kapur): trying a problem before being taught the idea beats the other order, as long as someone is there to help afterwards.
- **Human tutoring dialogue** (Graesser and Person): what expert tutors actually do turn by turn. Pumping, hinting, prompting and summarizing.
- **Chi et al. (2001):** the tutors whose students learned most were not the ones who explained most. They were the ones who got the student explaining.
- **Schema induction** (Gick and Holyoak, 1983): one worked example teaches you that example. Two that look different and work the same teach you the idea.
- **Feedback** (Hattie and Timperley, 2007): feedback on the task, the process or how you steer yourself helps. Feedback about you doesn't.
- **Guided instruction** (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006): leaving a learner to discover it unaided doesn't work.
